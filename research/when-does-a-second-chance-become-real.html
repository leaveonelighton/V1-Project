When Does a Second Chance Become Real?

Grace doesn't erase the past. It makes room for what comes next.

We use the word grace easily. But what does grace mean when another person's past makes us uncomfortable?

Grace doesn't say what happened didn't matter. It doesn't erase consequences. It doesn't call wrong right.

Grace doesn't erase the past. It makes room for what comes next.

A person makes a serious mistake. There are consequences. And there should be. But then something else happens. Years pass. They work. Learn. Raise families. Accept responsibility. Rebuild trust. Become dependable.

They become the people we say rehabilitation is supposed to produce. And still, somewhere, there is a record.

That record is real. But so is everything that happened afterward.

I can tell you what it looked like from my side.

I spent three months incarcerated. I lost my job. I lost the house my wife and I had lived in for twelve years. We lost benefits.

I completed a 24-week BIPP program. I completed eight hours of anger management. I completed alcohol and drug screening. I paid fines and court costs.

Then I came home and tried to do something most people do without thinking very much about it. I tried to work.

I applied for jobs and heard no. Then Uber said yes. For about three weeks, I drove. I was working again, earning again, moving forward again. And then that opportunity disappeared too.

There are details surrounding my case that matter. I'm not going to use this page to retry them. That's not why I'm telling you this story.

Finishing what the court requires and finishing the consequences aren't necessarily the same thing.

A consequence can have more than one address.

When someone's ability to earn a living disappears, the consequences don't necessarily stop with the person whose name appears on the record. They come home.

There is still housing to pay for. Electricity. Groceries. Medical needs. A spouse. Children. A family trying to keep going.

In my case, there was my wife. Whatever anyone believes about everything that came before my incarceration, making it harder for me to earn a living didn't affect only me. It affected our household.

If rehabilitation is something we genuinely want, what happens when someone tries to reenter ordinary life? Work is ordinary life. Paying bills is ordinary life. Providing for your family is ordinary life. Being trusted with responsibility again is ordinary life.

I still have anger about some of it.

I'd be lying if I told you otherwise. And maybe Give Grace has something to say to me about that, too.

My faith doesn't give me permission to spend the rest of my life keeping score against everyone I believe got something wrong. I'm still learning to release some of that. To forgive. To trust God with what I cannot change. And to ask what I can do with the things I can change.

This happened to me. Maybe knowing what it felt like will help us see somebody else differently.

Maybe understanding is where grace begins. Not agreement. Not excusing. Not forgetting. Understanding.

Then there was my friend.

I know a man with a criminal record. But that's not how I know him. I know the husband. The father. The man of faith. The career he built. The family he loves. The person he became.

He and his wife wanted to adopt a child. They believed they had room in their home and their family for one more. And an old part of his life became part of that conversation again.

I'm not going to tell his story for him. The details belong to him. And without knowing every legal fact, I'm not going to tell you whether an agency or law reached the right decision or the wrong one. Protecting children matters.

But I can tell you why his disappointment stayed with me. Because once, somebody intended to adopt me.

Peg and Jerry Gorman planned for me to enter their home through foster care and eventually be adopted. The legal adoption never happened. But we became family.

Somehow, I became their son anyway. And I became the first of 57 young people who would eventually come through that home.

So when this man I respect wanted to make room for another child, something about his story hit close to home.

When does a second chance become real?

I can't promise my friend an outcome. But I believe the person he became deserves to be part of the conversation about the person he once was.

And then I thought about employers.

A criminal record can contain information an employer legitimately needs to consider. Safety matters. Trust matters. The responsibilities of the particular job matter. Grace doesn't require abandoning good judgment. It requires using it.

Before an otherwise qualified applicant disappears because of an old record, there are questions worth asking: What actually happened? How long ago? Does it relate to this job? Is the information accurate? What happened afterward? What does the work history show? What do the references say?

Sometimes the answer should still be no. Grace doesn't require a yes. It asks us to make sure the no isn't automatic.

A background check is not a biography.

It may show an offense, a date, a disposition, maybe a sentence. But it cannot adequately describe twenty years of life afterward: jobs held, promotions earned, education completed, children raised, promises kept, trust rebuilt, people willing to put their names behind yours.

Those things don't erase an offense. But they are evidence too.

A second chance isn't asking someone to forget the evidence against you. It's asking them to consider the evidence for you, too.

What about the person you know?

Sometimes the first second chance is very small. An interview. A returned phone call. A reference. A ride to work. Help with a résumé. A conversation. Teaching somebody a skill. Making an introduction. Listening long enough to understand.

We will never ask you to do everything. We will only invite you to do something.

Grace goes both ways.

I can't ask other people to believe human beings can change while refusing to let grace change me. My faith tells me grace isn't merely something I hope to receive. It's something I'm called to give.

Maybe that's why this initiative isn't called Get Grace. It's called Give Grace.

One fair look.

You don't have to hire everyone with a record. Just don't let the record keep you from seeing the person.

See. Care. Act. Repeat.
