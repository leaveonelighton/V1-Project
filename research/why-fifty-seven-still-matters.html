First Light

Why Fifty-Seven Still Matters

Two separate meanings. One practical invitation.

Published August 3, 2026 · U.S. scope

Fifty-seven is not a slogan we went looking for. It was already part of the story.

At thirteen, I entered the foster home of Peg and Jerry Gorman at 5 Cedar Hill Road in Simsbury, Connecticut. I became the first of fifty-seven young people they would welcome over the years. That number is part of the founding history of Leave One Light On. It represents fifty-seven individual lives, not one interchangeable group, and it began with the choice of two ordinary people to make room for someone else. [Source: Leave One Light On, “The Founding Story.”]

Decades later, fifty-seven appeared in a different place. In July 2026, the U.S. Administration for Children and Families reported that, nationwide, there were 57 licensed foster homes for every 100 children in care. The agency uses that ratio as a national benchmark in its A Home for Every Child initiative. [Source: ACF, July 9, 2026.]

The two facts are separate. One belongs to the history of a particular home. The other is a current federal measure of foster-home capacity. They are not causally, prophetically, or statistically connected. But together they give us a useful reason to pause.

What does fifty-seven ask us to notice?

First, a national ratio is a signal, not a complete portrait. “57 licensed foster homes for every 100 children in care” does not mean that 43 children are homeless. It does not tell us whether a particular home is open, located near a child’s school and community, able to keep siblings together, or prepared for that child’s needs. It does tell us why capacity, recruitment, retention, kinship connections, and family-strengthening work deserve serious attention. ACF itself describes the measure as a ratio of licensed homes to children in care and plans more frequent state reporting. [Source: ACF, July 9, 2026.]

Second, a home is only one part of what a young person may need. Growing older does not make the need for dependable adults disappear. Young people moving toward adulthood may also be navigating education, housing, transportation, health care, identification documents, and employment. The federal National Youth in Transition Database follows outcomes at ages 17, 19, and 21 across areas including financial self-sufficiency, education, homelessness, health insurance, and positive connections with adults. [Source: GAO-25-107154, 2025.]

That is where careers and opportunity become part of practical hope.

A first job can be more than a paycheck. It can be a place to learn how a workplace works, build a reliable routine, earn a reference, discover a skill, and meet an adult who notices when something is wrong. A mentor can help translate an unfamiliar hiring process. A supervisor can make expectations clear. A coworker can explain the question everyone else seems to understand. A local training program can open a door that a young person did not know existed.

None of those actions requires turning a young person’s history into a label. Foster-care experience is not a prediction of someone’s character, ability, or future. Good support begins by seeing a whole person, asking what would actually help, and respecting the answer.

The federal Chafee program exists to support eligible young people currently or formerly in foster care as they move toward adulthood. According to the U.S. Government Accountability Office, the program provides approximately $186 million annually, and allowable support includes employment assistance as well as help with housing and other independent-living needs. GAO also found that states returned an average of about 5 percent of available Chafee and Education and Training Voucher funds from fiscal years 2018 through 2022, showing that available support and accessible support are not always the same thing. [Source: GAO-25-107154, 2025.]

That gap between what exists and what someone can actually reach is where practical people matter.

Opportunity also has to be understandable. A job posting may assume reliable transportation, a permanent mailing address, the right documents, interview clothing, and an adult who can explain what happens next. Training may be advertised without making the entry requirements clear. Even a promising connection can disappear when nobody follows up. We should not assume every young person faces these barriers, but we can remove unnecessary confusion from the opportunities we control. Clear instructions, realistic schedules, named points of contact, and respectful feedback cost little and help more than one kind of applicant.

An employer does not need to become a social-service agency. A professional does not need to promise a lifetime of mentorship. A neighbor does not need to know every part of the foster-care system. Each can still make one useful connection: review a résumé, explain an apprenticeship, help someone prepare for an interview, point toward a legitimate training provider, offer a job shadow, or become a dependable professional reference after trust has been earned.

This is the heart of Leave One Light On. We do not ask one person to solve everything. We invite many people to notice what is within reach and take one responsible step.

Fifty-seven still matters because numbers can become background noise unless we let them lead us back to people. The fifty-seven young people welcomed at 5 Cedar Hill Road had different stories. The federal ratio describes a national capacity challenge, not the future of any individual child. Both remind us that meaningful change is built one safe home, one steady relationship, one useful opportunity, and one kept promise at a time.

One practical action

Here is one practical action for today: offer one hour of career help. Review one résumé, conduct one practice interview, explain one path into your field, or connect one young person or transition program with a trustworthy local opportunity. Start small, be specific, and follow through.

Someone does not need every door opened at once. Sometimes one open door is enough to help them see the next one.
