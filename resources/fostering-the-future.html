Fostering the Future: Initiatives and Opportunities for Foster Youth

Hope and Practical Compassion

Research focus: First Lady Melania Trump's foster-care-related initiatives, the opportunities available, and the questions that still need answers.

Last reviewed November 10, 2026 · Hope and Practical Compassion

What happens after foster care?

A young person leaving foster care may need more than tuition: stable housing, transportation, guidance, work experience, a dependable adult, and the confidence that someone believes in their future. Public programs and private partnerships can help—but what matters most is whether young people can actually use the opportunities.

1. College scholarships: the LSU announcement

On November 7, 2026, Louisiana State University announced a $7.2 million endowed scholarship fund for students with foster-care experience. LSU says the fund includes a $5 million lead gift from MMR Group and contributions from other donors and is expected to generate approximately $288,000 annually. The school says it can support costs such as room and board, not only tuition and fees.

LSU reports that the national Fostering the Future network includes 26 partner colleges and universities. Those numbers describe an announced network and funding commitment; they do not establish how many students will ultimately graduate or the precise eligibility criteria for every school.

Read LSU's original announcement and follow its scholarship information.

2. Financial accounts: understand the $1,000 rule

The First Lady announced Fostering the Future Accounts in June 2026, building on the broader Trump Accounts framework.

Important eligibility distinction: A child may qualify for a Trump Account if they have a valid Social Security number and will not turn 18 before the end of the calendar year in which it is established. The separate, one-time $1,000 federal pilot contribution is limited to eligible U.S.-citizen children born January 1, 2025 through December 31, 2028 with a valid Social Security number, and the contribution must be elected. Not every child in foster care qualifies for this contribution.

The IRS now describes automatic enrollment for eligible children starting October 1, 2026. An authorized individual must still claim an account to manage it, and the $1,000 contribution requires a separate election. Account access and the federal contribution are different steps.

The IRS also states that a child-welfare agency serving as legal guardian may elect to establish an account for an eligible child, but the agency itself cannot elect the federal $1,000 pilot contribution. An authorized qualifying individual may be able to elect it where the requirements are met. Always consult the current IRS guidance before making decisions.

Check official IRS rules and agency-specific instructions.

3. Public-private partnerships

A November 2025 executive order directed federal work on Fostering the Future partnerships involving government, educational institutions, companies and nonprofit organizations, with a focus on educational, employment and other pathways for young people in or transitioning out of foster care. An executive order or announcement should not be mistaken for evidence that every planned service has been delivered. We will follow implementation and measurable results.

4. What research says young people need

A January 2025 U.S. Government Accountability Office report identified housing, postsecondary education and other needs among youth transitioning to adulthood. The GAO also found that states returned some federal Chafee and education-voucher funding while needs remained unmet. This illustrates why program accessibility, awareness and follow-through matter as much as announcements.

A reflection from founder Ted Johnson

I was the first official foster child welcomed into the Gorman family home. When I hear about efforts to help foster children build a financial future, I think about what even a thousand dollars might have meant when I was young.

But money alone doesn't replace belonging. A scholarship doesn't replace someone who believes in you. What matters to me is whether a young person finds an open door—and somebody willing to help them walk through it.

Commit to One: meaningful steps within reach

  • Share a verified scholarship or eligibility resource with a foster family or young adult.
  • Ask a trusted local organization whether mentoring, tutoring, or transport assistance is needed.
  • Help a young person prepare a résumé, scholarship application, or college checklist through an authorized program.
  • Support a foster family with a practical, specific need while respecting privacy and boundaries.

Notice the need. Take one meaningful step.
